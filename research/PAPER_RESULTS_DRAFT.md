# Draft Paper Section: Experimental Evaluation

> This is a draft grounded in the benchmark artifacts generated by the project on 25 October 2026. It reports synthetic proof-of-concept results only. It is not suitable for use as a claim of real-world superiority without the additional experiments listed below.

## Experimental setup

We evaluated five strategy-selection approaches: fixed Pearson correlation, fixed Spearman correlation, the existing skew-based heuristic, task-level majority voting with the application's abstain-to-heuristic fallback, and context-aware historical memory. The contextual method used the project's `SelfEvolutionEngine` recommendation implementation with versioned profiles derived from sample size, missingness, skewness, IQR-outlier fraction, the absolute Pearson–Spearman coefficient gap, and constant-column indicators.

The synthetic benchmark defined six scenario families: approximately linear data with light-tailed noise; linear data with missing completely at random (MCAR) values; linear data with smaller samples; monotonic exponential relationships; monotonic cubic relationships; and an underlying monotonic relationship with extreme response contamination, where the declared objective was robust rank association. Scenario sample size varied approximately ±15% around a base value, and noise and applicable perturbation rates varied by random seed. The scenario protocol specified a reference strategy per family. These labels represent a predeclared analysis objective for the synthetic scenario and should not be interpreted as a universal rule for choosing Pearson or Spearman.

For each run, the memory was seeded with 72 oracle-labelled synthetic experiences and evaluated on 180 queries generated with disjoint random seeds. Five seeds produced 900 held-out synthetic queries. The scenario families appeared in both training and testing, so this is held-out-sample evaluation within known scenario families, not unseen-family or real-world generalization. A task-majority tie abstained and fell back to the skew heuristic; the training labels were balanced (36 Pearson and 36 Spearman), so this comparator was operationally identical to the heuristic under this protocol.

## Results

Across the five runs, contextual memory achieved 97.7% mean strategy-selection accuracy (standard deviation 0.2 percentage points; 95% Student-t interval across seeds [97.4%, 98.0%]). The skew-based heuristic achieved 82.0% (standard deviation 0.3 points; interval [81.6%, 82.4%]). The mean paired difference was +15.7 percentage points in favor of contextual memory, with a 95% t interval of [15.1, 16.2] percentage points across the five seed-level comparisons. Fixed Pearson and fixed Spearman each achieved 50.0% overall accuracy because the six reference scenarios were evenly divided between Pearson and Spearman objectives.

Per-scenario averages showed that contextual memory performed particularly well in the outlier-contaminated monotonic scenario (100.0% vs 22.7% for the skew heuristic) and improved selection on the monotonic cubic scenario (87.3% vs 76.0%). It achieved 98.7% on the small-sample linear scenario, while the skew heuristic achieved 93.3%. Contextual-memory coverage was 100% within this constructed evaluation, because every test context had sufficiently similar labelled experiences under the configured retrieval threshold. Coverage is expected to change with less similar or previously unseen data.

A separate stress test added 20 copies of one Pearson execution with an identical dataset, column pair, and rounded context profile. Raw task-level counts shifted from a tie (36 Pearson, 36 Spearman) to a Pearson majority (56 vs 36). Contextual memory returned the same recommendation and support before and after these replays, consistent with its repeated-context grouping mechanism. The repeated copies were not independent observations and this was a deliberately constructed stress test.

## Limitations

The current results support further study of context-aware retrieval, but do not establish real-world superiority. The training experiences were labelled from the synthetic scenario's declared reference method; the experiment did not test whether the application's structural-verification flag can independently identify statistically high-quality strategies. Because scenario families appear in both training and testing, the results do not measure transfer to unseen scenario families. In the within-family repeated-seed protocol, the task-majority comparator tied and fell back to the heuristic because of the balanced training pool, so it added no independent evidence beyond that baseline. In LOFO, we now distinguish the dataset-scoped historical fallback (which correctly abstains for unseen dataset IDs and falls back to the heuristic) from a separate global-majority diagnostic (which gets 0% due to the split's class-construction artifact). Coefficient-recovery error is reported as a supplementary metric against large clean reference samples, but Pearson and Spearman estimate different association quantities and their errors should not be interpreted as a universal quality ranking.

Before a conference performance claim, the benchmark should be expanded to leave-one-family-out tests, additional random seeds, independently sourced real datasets, objective-specific quality labels, and ablations of the profile features, duplicate grouping, similarity threshold, and abstention policy. The exact seeds, per-case CSV, aggregate JSON, plots, and command lines are archived under `outputs/research_evaluation/`.

## Leave-one-synthetic-family-out transfer experiment

To assess transfer beyond scenario families seen during memory construction, we added a leave-one-synthetic-family-out (LOFO) protocol. For each fold, one of the six defined families was excluded entirely from the experience graph; the remaining five families contributed 12 oracle-labelled experiences each. The framework was evaluated on 30 newly generated cases from the excluded family, repeated over five random seeds. This resulted in 30 folds and 900 test cases. Each fold used a fresh graph, so the held-out family did not contribute historical examples to its own test fold.

Across seeds, contextual memory achieved 84.0% mean selection accuracy (SD 1.7 percentage points; range 81.1–85.6%), compared with 80.8% for the skew-based heuristic (SD 0.8 points; range 80.0–81.7%). Fixed Pearson and fixed Spearman each achieved 50.0%. We report two distinct experience baselines to avoid conflating their semantics. The dataset-scoped historical recommender had no eligible experience for the newly named held-out test datasets and therefore fell back to the skew heuristic, also achieving 80.8%. A separate global-majority-with-heuristic-fallback diagnostic achieved 0.0% in this protocol because the reference labels are distributed across three Pearson and three Spearman families: excluding any single family leaves the held-out class as the minority among the other five families, so a context-free global majority systematically selects the opposite label. This is an artifact of the benchmark's balanced family construction and must not be presented as a realistic expectation for dataset-scoped historical memory.

Family-level performance was mixed rather than uniformly improved. Contextual memory scored 90.0% on the held-out clean-linear family compared with 100.0% for the skew heuristic, while it scored 42.0% on the outlier-contaminated monotonic family compared with 24.7% for the heuristic. These outcomes suggest some transfer benefit for the harder outlier family but also show that contextual memory can degrade performance on a family where the heuristic already works well. Recommendation coverage was 100% for the LOFO queries; therefore the current similarity threshold did not abstain for any cases, even though the family was unseen during memory construction. This warrants a dedicated calibration experiment for out-of-distribution abstention.

These results remain synthetic and use oracle-labelled historical experiences for non-held-out families. Folds overlap in their training families, and only six scenario families have been examined; across-seed intervals should be treated as descriptive. The experiment does not establish real-world generalization or statistical superiority. Real datasets with independent quality criteria and feature-ablation experiments are still required.


## Cross-dataset real-data perturbation study (exploratory pilot)

We added a real-data perturbation benchmark using four documented datasets: scikit-learn's Diabetes dataset and the UCI Wine Recognition, Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic), and Iris datasets. Class or outcome labels were excluded from numeric feature-pair construction. For each dataset fold, the entire held-out dataset was excluded from the experience graph; training experiences came only from the other datasets. Up to 20 numeric feature pairs were sampled per dataset (all six pairs for Iris), giving 66 distinct feature pairs. Three random seeds were used, with two replicas per feature-pair and perturbation family, producing 792 perturbed test queries.

This is a narrowly defined robustness study, not an assessment of scientific truth. For each feature pair and perturbation family, the evaluation target was the estimator with the lower mean absolute coefficient shift from that estimator's own clean-data coefficient. If the difference was below an absolute 0.005 shift margin, the case was labelled ambiguous and excluded from selection-accuracy scoring. Perturbations were 10% missing-completely-at-random values independently masked in the two variables and 5% of rows receiving a large signed extreme outlier in the second variable. The target label was calculated for held-out cases only for evaluation; it was never added to that fold's memory.

Across held-out datasets and seeds, fixed Spearman achieved 84.6% mean dataset-macro agreement with the empirical stability target, with mean absolute coefficient shift 0.0318. Task-level majority with heuristic fallback matched the same metrics, indicating that the training labels favoured Spearman overall. Contextual memory reached 83.3% agreement and a mean absolute shift of 0.0319; therefore it did not outperform the simpler fixed-Spearman or task-majority baseline on this pilot. The skew heuristic reached 34.0% agreement with a mean shift of 0.0787. These scores indicate that the predeclared outlier-contamination objective strongly favoured Spearman; they do not establish Spearman as universally preferable.

Results were perturbation-dependent. Under MCAR missingness, contextual memory reached 49.3% agreement, versus 53.7% for fixed Spearman and task-majority voting. Under extreme-outlier contamination, fixed Spearman, task-majority voting, and contextual memory each reached 99.1%, while the skew heuristic reached 29.0% and fixed Pearson 0.9%. The contextual recommender made a recommendation for 100% of pilot queries, so this experiment did not exercise its abstention path and cannot establish out-of-distribution calibration.

This pilot therefore supplies an important negative finding: the current contextual-memory strategy has not yet demonstrated an advantage over a simple fixed-Spearman baseline under this robustness objective. It can outperform the skew heuristic on these tested perturbations, but the comparison is not sufficient for a novelty or superiority claim. The dataset count is small, the perturbations are controlled rather than naturally occurring, labels are derived from a predefined stability metric, and the scikit-learn dataset variants and versions should be recorded. The bundled Titanic sample was excluded because its provenance is not documented in the repository. Future work should improve objective-specific strategy memory, include perturbation families where Pearson is expected to be more stable, test whether memory abstains on genuinely dissimilar contexts, expand independent datasets, and report pair-clustered uncertainty estimates.
